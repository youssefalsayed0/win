Words first

Three money words

📥

Money in

Everything clients pay us for projects and support. Experts call it revenue.

📤

Money out

Salaries, cloud servers, software licenses, office rent. Experts call it cost.

🏦

What we keep

Money in minus money out. Experts call it profit.

CodeNest: 100M in, 42M out, so we keep 100M − 42M = 58M.
Ask a student to say the three words back in their own language. Ask: which one is bigger in a software house, salaries or office rent? Salaries.
Words first

Six more words

Client
A company or person who pays us to build software.
Project price
What one client pays for one project.
Fixed cost
Same every month, like office rent and software licenses.
Variable cost
Grows with every project, like developer hours and cloud servers.
Profit margin
The share of our income that we keep. For us: 58M out of 100M, which is 58%.
Budget
The most we agree to spend, decided before we start.
Fixed versus variable is the idea students find hardest. Use this: the office rent is paid even if we have zero projects. Developer time is only needed when there is a project.

Our costs: 12M for the company (office, tools, managers) plus 0.6M for each project (developers, testers, servers). At 50 projects that is 12M + 30M = 42M.

Keep the spending under control

Developers and testers 26M
Tools 5M
Sales 7M
4M
An example of where the 42M goes. The last piece is office and admin.
  • Write down every cost: salaries, servers, licenses.
  • One owner for each cost line.
  • Check every week: did we spend more than the plan?
  • Cancel tools that nobody uses.
  • Hire only for signed work, not for work we hope to get.
Ask: if a team pays for ten software subscriptions and uses six, what should they do? Cancel four. Small leaks sink big ships.
Puzzle 2 · Teamwork

A great software team has five habits

  • One goal: launch the app on the agreed date.
  • One name per task: every card on the board has an owner.
  • A 10-minute stand-up every day: what I did, what I will do, what blocks me.
  • A shared board that everyone can see, like Trello or Jira.
  • Report bugs and problems early, with no blaming.
Ask: what happens if two developers both think the other one is fixing the login page? Nobody fixes it. That is why every task has one name.

QA testers

One rule: testers never check only their own work. Fresh eyes find more bugs.
In small teams one person can do two jobs, but a developer should not be the only tester of their own code. Ask students who is the best at finding mistakes in their own homework: usually someone else.
Puzzle 2 · Teamwork

How do we know a project went well? A report card.

  • On time (30%). Did we launch on the agreed date?
  • Quality (25%). How many bugs did the client find after launch?
  • Within budget (20%). Did hours and costs stay in the plan?
  • Happy client (25%). Did the app solve their problem?

We agree on the percentages before the project starts, not after.

Final score

Try giving 100 to three subjects and 20 to quality. The score drops, which shows that every subject matters. Ask why a fast launch full of bugs is not a good result.
Puzzle 2 · Teamwork

Team first, then you

Team result 70%
Your part 30%

Keep

What worked. We do it again.

Stop

What wasted time or money.

Start

One new habit for the next project.

After every project, the team holds a short meeting and answers Keep, Stop, Start. Software teams call it a retrospective.

Why 70 and 30? If only the team counts, someone can relax. If only you count, nobody helps others. The mix keeps both. Your part means your own tasks, your code quality and the help you gave.
Puzzle 3 · Bonuses

The trap: a bonus only for selling more

The risky way

Our year: sales 100M, cost 42M, we keep 58M.

To hit their targets, sales people give clients a 20% discount. Sales become 80M. The developers still write the same code, so cost stays 42M. We keep only 38M.

Sales counted. Profit shrank.

A better idea

Pay bonuses only from the extra profit, the profit above our plan.

Discounts no longer help, because they shrink the profit the bonus is made from.

The bonus pays for itself.

Walk through 80M minus 42M equals 38M. Ask: if you were in sales and paid per contract, would you give the discount? Why? Then show how the new rule removes the temptation.
Puzzle 3 · Bonuses

The bonus recipe

💼

Fair salary

Normal pay. It is already inside the 42M.

🏆

Team bonus

Made only from extra profit.

🎓

Growth and thanks

Training courses, certificates, tech events, flexible hours, a shout-out at the demo day. Cheap and powerful.

Bonus pool = 15% of the extra profit

Two checks before any bonus: (1) Profit check: we keep at least 58% of the money coming in. (2) Quality check: the project report card is 70 or more.

Explain the two checks as gates: both must be open. The salary is already inside our spending, so it does not change our margin. The quality check stops a team from rushing a buggy launch to earn a bonus.
Puzzle 3 · Rewards for clients

Client rewards that pay for themselves

  • Referral reward. A client brings a new client and gets 5–10% of the new client's first payment, or service credit.
  • Renewal or expansion points. Paid after a contract renews or a new phase is added. The client swaps them for support hours, training or an extra feature.
  • Pay upfront or yearly. We get cash early and spend less on chasing payments. In return: a small discount on maintenance, extra hours or a performance report.

Example: the referral reward

New contract: 10,000. Margin on it: 4,000.

The reward of 500 comes out of the new client's margin, so we keep 3,500. The margin from our old clients is untouched.

500
We keep 3,500
The key idea: the reward is paid from money that did not exist before, the new client's margin. Ask: why is it important that the old clients' margin is not touched? Because then the reward cannot hurt what we already earn. The numbers here are one example contract, not the yearly totals.
Puzzle 3 · Rewards for clients

Client rewards that cost almost nothing

⭐

Perks, not cash

Priority support. A dedicated account manager. A training workshop for the client's team. Early access to new features. A short free architecture review.

🎬

Case study

The client lets us publish a case study or a video. Their reward: service credit or shared exposure. Cheap for us, and it brings new clients.

💝

Donate the points

The client collects points, then donates them. Low cost for us, high meaning for them.

Careful: keep perks short, so they do not eat hours we could sell to other clients.
Ask students what they would choose as a client: priority support or a small discount? Many choose support. It costs us less than money. The donation idea shows that rewards do not have to be about the client's own pocket.
Puzzle 3 · Rewards for the team

Rewards for the team

  • Share of project margin. After the client pays, share part of the extra margin. If the team saves 20 hours, 30–50% of the value of that saving becomes the reward.
  • Code reuse. Ready components, internal libraries and templates. Each reuse saves hours, so part of the saving is the reward.
  • Fewer bugs and less support. Fewer support tickets after launch and less rework.
  • Employee referral. Paid after the new hire passes the trial period, and funded by the hiring cost we saved.
  • Perks, not cash. Extra leave, a paid course or certificate, a device or tool, a conference, flexible work.
Notice the pattern: every reward comes from a saving or from new margin. Ask: why do we pay the employee referral only after the trial period? Because we only know the hire was a good one after that.

Puzzle 3 · Make the program pay for itself

Six ways to fund rewards without hurting margin

Premium support subscription
The client pays a monthly fee, and the rewards are paid from it.
Prepaid hour packs
The client buys 100 hours at a slightly lower price. We get steady cash.
Points that expire
Points that are never used lower the real cost. Finance people call this breakage.
Reward with a minimum
"10 free hours when you renew 100 hours."
High-margin work only
No points on low-margin projects.
Savings fund
Part of any project saving goes to team rewards.
Each of these answers the same question: where does the money for the reward come from? If you cannot name the source, do not offer the reward. Points that expire must be fair and clearly explained to clients.
Puzzle 3 · Golden rules

Golden rules for a software house

  • No general discount on the development price.
  • Never spend billable hours on rewards.
  • Reward the right behavior: referral, renewal, upfront payment, bigger scope, case study, less support.
  • Start by hand: Google Sheets and codes. Automate later.
  • Watch: referral rate, renewal rate, average contract size, net margin.

The safety test

Reward cost ÷ extra margin we expect

If the answer is less than 1, the reward is safe.

Example: reward 500, expected extra margin 4,000. 500 ÷ 4,000 = 0.125. Safe.

The safety test is the most useful tool of this section. Ask a student to try it with a reward of 3,000 and an expected extra margin of 2,000. The answer is 1.5, which is above 1, so the reward is not safe.
